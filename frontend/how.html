How we measure

Every number in Speech Coach has a written rule and a named source. Read them here.

1. What counts as a filler

Two kinds of words count. Hesitation sounds are always fillers. Verbal habits count only in context.

WordCounts when
um, uh, er, ah, hm Always, at any length: umm, uhh, erm, hmmm.
mhm, uh-huh, mm-hmm Always. Agreeing with yourself fills a gap just like um.
“like” A comma or um sets it off, or a 0.25 s pause comes before it mid-sentence. At the start of a sentence, a comma or um must follow it.
“you know” A comma or stop, an um, or a 0.25 s pause follows “know”.
“so” An um or a half-second hang follows it.
“actually”, “basically” They open a sentence or clause: first word, after a comma or stop, or right after another filler.

These never count:

  • A lone “huh”.
  • “like” right after I, you, we, they, would, to, do, don’t, not, really, just, looks, sounds, feels, seems, something, things, stuff, more or much. “I, um, like this plan” does not count.
  • “you know” right after do, does, did, don’t, if, would, let or as. “I’ll let you know.” does not count. “You know the drill.” counts only with a pause after “know”.
  • “So the impact is…”, “So, the impact is…” and “so far”, said without a hang. “I think so.” never counts.
  • “actually” or “basically” in mid-clause, as in “It is actually true.”

The reason: a coach that flags “I like this plan” teaches you to ignore it. A flag you can trust is a flag you act on.

2. Fillers per minute

Fillers per minute is every filler divided by your speaking time. Speaking time runs from your first word to your last.

The line is 3 a minute. Zoom Revenue Accelerator lists “0.6-3.0 filler words per minute”. tl;dv publishes the same range.

Fillers are a clarity signal, not a verdict. Gong found no link between filler rate and closed deals.

3. Words per minute

Hesitation sounds are not words, so they do not count here. Microsoft’s patent application and tl;dv remove silence from their rates. Leaving out ums applies the same idea to your word count. Pace is your words, minus hesitations, over your speaking time.

  • Microsoft Speaker Coach recommends 100 to 165 wpm.
  • Lecturers average about 145 wpm. TED speakers average about 169 (Wingrove).

Each passage has its own target band. These bands are guidance, not a score. The default band, 140 to 160, is the presenting rate Denise Graveline suggests. A little fast is not the problem. In research Trouvain summarizes, listeners found slightly faster second-language speech easier to understand. Only clearly too-fast speech lost points.

4. Pauses

A mid-sentence pause counts when it lasts 1.5 s or more. Mid-sentence means it does not follow a period, question mark or exclamation point. After a sentence ends, only a long pause of 5 s or more counts.

The 1.5 s bar is our choice. No coach app publishes a standard for it.

Pauses are not the problem. Listeners remember a word better after a silent pause (MacGregor and colleagues). What matters is where the pause falls.

In a listening study, a silence inside a clause sounded less fluent than the same silence at a clause boundary (Houghton and colleagues, 2024). Your transcript marks only the pauses the card counts, mid-sentence or long.

A pause of 5 s or more counts wherever it falls, even after a period. A freeze that long is lost time, not a pause that lets a point land. The card counts it, and the transcript and speech map mark it as a long pause.

5. Longest clean stretch

This is your longest run of speech with none of these:

  • A filler.
  • A mid-sentence pause.
  • A pause of 5 s or more, wherever it falls.
  • A restart, where you start a phrase over.

Breaks shape how fluent you sound. When untrained listeners rate fluency, pausing and speed explain most of their judgment (Bosker and colleagues, 2013).

15 clean seconds is our practice target for beginners. Below it, Results may ask you to stretch your clean run. The Path’s Long pauses unit starts at 15 seconds and builds to a full minute.

6. The tips

You see at most two tips. Each fires on a rule. If none fires, you see a clean-run note. The tips show first. Coach notes take their place when they arrive.

TipShows when
Above target pace Pace is more than 15 wpm above the top of the passage band.
Under target pace Pace is more than 15 wpm below the bottom of the passage band.
Fillers More than 3 fillers a minute. The tip lists up to three times.
Habit Your most used verbal habit appears 3 times or more.
Mid-sentence pause A mid-sentence pause runs past 3 seconds.
Freeze A pause between sentences lasts 5 seconds or more.
Restarts You restart phrases 3 times or more.

Each tip names moments in your take and one technique to try. High-information feedback averaged d = 0.99 across 435 studies (Wisniewski and colleagues, 2019). Brief habit-reversal training cut speech disfluencies in college speakers.

7. Coach notes

After each take, Results shows two or three coach notes. Each note draws on one entry of a research library of 632 entries. Every entry names its source: a study, a book, a coach or an organization. A peer-reviewed source carries a “Peer reviewed” badge.

Claude, an AI model from Anthropic, writes the notes. It rewrites two or three entries for your take, in your numbers. It is told to use only the entries and your numbers. The app, not the model, attaches each source.

To write them, the app sends Anthropic two things. Your take’s numbers, and the first 1,500 characters of your transcript. It never sends audio. It never sends your email.

  • When the AI is off, the library’s own words stand in, still cited. The same happens once its hourly budget is spent. The line above the notes says which you got.
  • An entry you have seen is not shown again while new ones remain.
  • Tap a source to read it. The link opens in a new tab.

8. What these numbers cannot tell you

  • Transcription can miss a quiet um.
  • “I, like, think” is missed on purpose. Flagging a real verb costs more trust than missing one filler.
  • Background noise, accents and speech above about 250 wpm lower accuracy.

Treat the numbers as a guide to listen again, not a grade.

The Path

The Path has four units: Restarts, Fillers, Long pauses and Pace. Each trains one skill over 5 levels. Your weakest unit comes first.

A level is one short loop:

StepWhat happens
Goal One target, such as 6 or fewer fillers a minute. Examples show what to aim for.
Take 1 Your first try. It does not count.
Feedback Your number, the change since your last take and one tip with its source.
Review About 30 seconds on up to 3 moments from take 1. You can replay each moment and pick one fix.
Take 2 The same topic or passage again. This is the take that counts.
Result Level cleared, or not yet, with the one fix to try next.

A few levels count differently. Restarts levels 1 and 2 have three reads. Read 3 is the one that counts. On Pace level 2, both reads count.

Take 1's audio stays in this phone's memory for this level only, then it is deleted. It is never saved.

You get 3 scored tries at a level each day. After 3 misses, it rests until tomorrow.

A cleared level comes back as a check-back: one quick take on new material. Check-back 1 is due 2 days after you clear the level. Check-back 2 is due about a week after you clear it.

  • Pass check-back 1, and the node gets a half ring.
  • Pass both, and the node turns gold: “Locked in”.
  • Miss one, and it comes back 2 days later. Nothing locks.

Crowns are optional challenges that open after level 3. Fillers has sudden death, and Pace has a 180 wpm ladder. You never need a crown to move on.

Each unit ends with a checkpoint: 2 minutes on a new topic. You can do it with someone listening in the room.

Every 14 days, the Path asks for a benchmark. You read the same passage and talk on the same prompt each time. It takes about 3 minutes and shows your real progress. It never clears or locks a level.

The Path and its levels show no rank. Your rank is on Progress and Results.

The Path is built on tested practice methods. We know of no study that tests a level path like this for speaking. The tested parts are inside each level: practice, feedback, review and a retake. Each source opens in a new tab.

  • Say it again. Restarts fade last: in one study they dropped only after the 4th repeat. Source: Craig Lambert, Judit Kormos, Danny Minn, Task Repetition and Second Language Speech Processing (2017)
  • Say it again right away. Even one immediate repeat helps: speakers in one study talked faster the second time. Source: Gavin Bui, Mohammad Javad Ahmadian, Ann-Marie Hunter, Spacing effects on repeated L2 task performance (2019)
  • Say it in less time. Retelling a talk in less time on each repeat brought gains in speed and pausing. They lasted on a later test. Source: Mai Ngoc Tran, Kazuya Saito, Effects of the 4/3/2 Activity Revisited: Extending Boers (2014) and Thai & Boers (2016) (n.d.)
  • Notice, then pause. Noticing your fillers and pausing silently in their place cut disfluencies. That held for all four speakers in one study. Source: Brittany Pawlik, Christopher J. Perrin, Reducing speech disfluencies during public speaking using brief habit reversal (2020)
  • Come back later. Just learning to spot your own fillers can reduce them. In one study, booster sessions were needed for further gains. Source: Claire Spieler, Raymond Miltenberger, Using awareness training to decrease nervous habits during public speaking (2017)
  • Train what listeners hear. When untrained listeners rate fluency, pausing and speed explain most of their judgment. Source: Hans Rutger Bosker, Anne-France Pinget, Hugo Quené, Ted Sanders, Nivja H. de Jong, What makes speech sound fluent? The contributions of pauses, speed and repairs (2013)

Most of these studies are small, and several tested second-language speakers. Each level also shows one tip from the research library, with its source.
