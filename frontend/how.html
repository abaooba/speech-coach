How we measure

Every number in Speech Coach has a written rule and a named source. Read them here.

1. What counts as a filler

Two kinds of words count. Hesitation sounds are always fillers. Verbal habits count only in context.

WordCounts when
um, uh, er, ah, hm Always, at any length: umm, uhh, erm, hmmm.
“like” A comma or um sets it off, or a 0.25 s pause comes before it mid-sentence. At the start of a sentence, a comma or um must follow it.
“you know” A comma or stop, an um, or a 0.25 s pause follows “know”.
“so” An um or a half-second hang follows it.
“actually”, “basically” They open a sentence or clause: first word, after a comma or stop, or right after another filler.

These never count:

  • Agreement sounds: mhm, uh-huh, mm-hmm and huh.
  • “like” right after I, you, we, they, would, to, do, don’t, not, really, just, looks, sounds, feels, seems, something, things, stuff, more or much. “I, um, like this plan” does not count.
  • “you know” right after do, does, did, don’t, if, would, let or as. “I’ll let you know.” does not count. “You know the drill.” counts only with a pause after “know”.
  • “So the impact is…”, “So, the impact is…” and “so far”, said without a hang. “I think so.” never counts.
  • “actually” or “basically” in mid-clause, as in “It is actually true.”

The reason: a coach that flags “I like this plan” teaches you to ignore it. A flag you can trust is a flag you act on.

2. Fillers per minute

Fillers per minute is every filler divided by your speaking time. Speaking time runs from your first word to your last.

The line is 3 a minute. Zoom Revenue Accelerator lists “0.6-3.0 filler words per minute”. tl;dv publishes the same range.

Fillers are a clarity signal, not a verdict. Gong found no link between filler rate and closed deals.

3. Words per minute

Hesitation sounds are not words, so they do not count here. Microsoft’s patent application and tl;dv remove silence from their rates. Leaving out ums applies the same idea to your word count. Pace is your words, minus ums and uhs, over your speaking time.

  • Microsoft Speaker Coach recommends 100 to 165 wpm.
  • Lecturers average about 145 wpm. TED speakers average about 169 (Wingrove).

Each passage has its own target band. These bands are guidance, not a score. The NSDA World Schools norms say it plainly: “There should be no ‘spreading’ in this format.”

4. Mid-sentence pauses

A pause counts when it lasts 1.5 s or more and does not follow a period, question mark or exclamation point.

The 1.5 s bar is our choice. No coach app publishes a standard for it.

Pauses are not the problem. Listeners remember a word better after a silent pause (MacGregor and colleagues). What matters is where the pause falls.

NSDA’s “I Spy Fluency Breaks” drill hunts “fluency breaks or unnecessarily long pauses”. Your transcript marks those pauses for you.

5. Longest clean stretch

This is your longest run of speech with none of these:

  • A filler.
  • A mid-sentence pause.
  • A restart, where you start a phrase over.

The goal comes from debate drills. NSDA’s Start Stops and Perfect Point drills aim for speech without breaks. DebateDrills gives beginners 15 clean seconds.

6. The tips

You see at most two tips. Each fires on a rule. If none fires, you see a clean-run note.

TipShows when
Above target pace Pace is more than 15 wpm above the top of the passage band.
Under target pace Pace is more than 15 wpm below the bottom of the passage band.
Fillers More than 3 fillers a minute. The tip lists up to three times.
Habit Your most used verbal habit appears 3 times or more.
Long pause A mid-sentence pause runs past 3 seconds.
Restarts You restart phrases 3 times or more.

Each tip names moments in your take and one technique to try. High-information feedback averaged d = 0.99 across 435 studies (Wisniewski and colleagues, 2019). Brief habit-reversal training cut speech disfluencies in college speakers.

7. What these numbers cannot tell you

  • Transcription can miss a quiet um.
  • “I, like, think” is missed on purpose. Flagging a real verb costs more trust than missing one filler.
  • Background noise, accents and speech above about 250 wpm lower accuracy.

Treat the numbers as a guide to listen again, not a grade.
