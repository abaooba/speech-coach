# Does a Duolingo-style skill path help people speak better?

Research sweep run on 2026-10-13 for the Path tab: five researchers read the sources, one wrote the synthesis, and two fact-checkers opened every citation. 55 citations: 51 verified as cited, 4 corrected (marked below).

## Verdict

No. No study has tested a Duolingo-style skill path (units, levels, unlocks, XP, streaks) for speaking, so the Path is not "scientifically proven" to help speaking, and the app should not claim it is.

The closest test is a gamified public-speaking app for 13 to 16 year olds (S3). It had 15 VR levels, and you needed 4 of 5 stars to unlock the next one:
- Right after treatment it beat doing nothing (d = 0.61).
- It was not better than a plain online lesson (p = .056).
- At 3 months no group was ahead of another.
- At 12 months the group still improving was the plain lesson plus real-life speaking tasks (S4).

The wider evidence on games and on Duolingo:
- In classroom studies, game features give small to medium gains. They are steadiest on knowledge tests. Skill outcomes, which is where speaking belongs, show the smallest and least stable effect, g = 0.25 (S1, S2).
- Duolingo's own speaking numbers have no comparison group (S6).
- In a Duolingo-funded study, time spent in the app went with written gains but not oral gains (S5).

What does have support is what goes inside a path:
- one specific goal at a time (S15, S16)
- feedback that says what went wrong and how to fix it (S17)
- giving the same talk again (S24, S25, S26)
- spreading practice over days (S27, S28)
- learning to notice your own fillers (S40, S41)
- reviewing your recording against a checklist (S34)

So the path is a good container for practice, not the thing that makes you better.

## What to build: the Loop Path

Change it, don't replace it. The research does not offer a different overall method with better proof for speaking, so keep the winding Option A path and the four units. What changes is what a level is and how you pass it.

1. **Each level becomes a 5 to 7 minute loop.** You see one goal with an example, do take 1, then get specific feedback: your number, the change since your last take, three timestamped moments, and one cited tip. You spend 30 seconds reviewing those moments, then do take 2 on the same prompt. Take 2 is the one that counts.
2. **Cleared levels come back as one-take "check-backs" on new material, 2 and 7 days later.** Passing the 7-day one turns the node gold. Failing never locks anything again.
3. **Pass rules use rates, not zero.** Fillers step down to 5 a minute, where listeners stopped marking speakers down, and finish at the app's existing 3 a minute.
4. **Every pass also checks that your other slips didn't rise and that you kept talking.** Nobody can win by swapping ums for freezes or by saying very little.
5. **The pressure modes become optional.** Sudden death and the 180 wpm ladder turn into crown challenges.
6. **The streak forgives one missed day a week.**
7. **The rank badge moves out of the loop and off the Path header** and onto Progress.
8. **Each unit ends with a 2-minute new-topic checkpoint**, which you can do in front of a real listener.

Two more things:
- In the app and the Challenge write-up, describe it as "built on tested practice methods", not "scientifically proven".
- Add a benchmark every 14 days: the same held-out passage and prompt each time. Then the app can show whether it is actually working.

To your question: yes, go with this version. The pages to generate are the Path, the Level loop and the Review step.

### One level, step by step

A session is one check-back (if one is due) plus one level loop. It takes about 5 to 8 minutes, aiming for 4 practice days a week.

Baseline: your last 5 takes, weighted by speaking minutes as path.order already does, give your starting numbers: fillers per minute, restarts per minute, short mid-sentence pauses per minute, 5 s+ pauses per minute, wpm and clean stretch. For a brand-new speaker, take 1 of the first loop is the baseline.

1. Check-back (if due, about 90 s). One 60 to 90 s take on new material at the target of a level you cleared 2 or 7 days ago. New material means a new topic, or an unseen cold read for Restarts. A pass fills the node's ring: half at +2 days, gold ('locked in') at +7. A miss comes back in 2 days, and nothing locks.

2. Goal card (about 15 s). One number and one length (for example '5 or fewer fillers a minute, 90 s'), a two-line example and non-example, and the unit's cited 'why this works' line.

3. Take 1 (60 to 120 s, set by the level). Practice only; it is the source for the review.

4. Feedback (about 15 s). Your number against the target, the change from your last take of this skill, up to 3 timestamped moments with the words around them, and one cited tip from the library for this unit. No rank badge.

5. Review (30 to 45 s). For each moment, choose what happened (lost my point, searching for a word, nerves, planned breath), then choose one fix for take 2.

6. Take 2, on the same prompt or passage. Pace and Long-pauses levels may shorten it, 4/3/2 style. This take is scored.

7. Result.
- Pass: the next level opens now, and check-backs are booked for +2 and +7 days.
- Not yet: the screen names the one fix. You may do takes 3 and 4 now; after that the level waits until tomorrow.

Guards. Every pass also needs all three:
- (a) You talked for at least 80% of the set time. Reading levels use the existing read-it-through rules instead.
- (b) Talk levels: at least 120 wpm.
- (c) Your other slips per minute rose no more than 1.0 above your baseline, leaving out the unit's own skill. Slips are weighted as in the rank: filler 1, restart 1.5, short mid-sentence pause 1, 5 s+ pause 3.

Jump test: one take that meets a later level's target clears every level below it. Their check-backs still come.

Unit checkpoint (after level 5): 2 minutes on a new Impromptu topic, no prep, scored on everything. It needs the unit's level-5 target and total weighted slips per minute at or below your baseline. 'In the room' version: record it with at least one person listening; it earns its own mark.

Benchmark (day 1, then every 14 days): the same held-out cold-read passage and the same Impromptu prompt, never used anywhere else, so Progress shows real change outside the path.

Optional crowns (never required to move on):
- after Fillers level 3: Sudden death, 30, 45, 60, 90 or 120 s
- after Pace level 3: Speed ladder at 180 ± 10 wpm with at most 1 restart

### Units

Fillers (um, uh, mhm and habit words, counted per minute; talk levels). The levels follow the tested awareness-training steps.
- L1 Name it: 60 s x 2 on 'Your day'. The goal card lists your top 3 fillers from past takes, and you confirm them. Pass on take 2: fillers per minute at or below halfway between your baseline and 8 (or 8, if you are already under it).
- L2 Spot it: 60 s x 2. In the review, replay take 1 and tap each filler you hear before the app shows them ('You caught 4 of 6'). Pass: 8 or fewer a minute.
- L3 Catch it live: 90 s x 2 on an Impromptu topic, with an optional big tap target to mark a filler the moment you hear yourself say it. Pass: 6 or fewer a minute.
- L4 Pause instead: 90 s x 2. When you feel an um coming, close your lips and pause briefly, under 1.5 s, which the app does not count as a pause. Pass: 5 or fewer a minute, the rate listeners did not mark down.
- L5 New topic: 120 s x 2 on a topic you have not used. Pass: 3 or fewer a minute, the app's existing 'Target under 3'.

Check-backs: one 60 to 90 s take on a new topic at that level's rate. Checkpoint: 2 minutes on a new topic at 3 or fewer a minute, with weighted slips at or below baseline. Crown after L3: Sudden death.

Why this works: noticing your own ums and pausing instead cut fillers for nearly every speaker in 7 small single-case studies (S40, S41, S43; in S43 the gain carried over to a class talk for 1 of 4 speakers; library filler-087, filler-088, filler-085), and zero isn't needed (S46; filler-094).

Restarts (restarts plus misreads per passage on reading levels; restarts per minute in talk).
- L1 Read it 3 times: a new 120 to 200 word cold-read passage, read 3 times in a row (about 60 to 80 s each). The review after read 1 lists each restart with the words you went back over. Pass on read 3: 4 or fewer slips.
- L2 Read it 3 times, new passage. Pass on read 3: 3 or fewer.
- L3 Read it twice, new passage. Pass on read 2: 2 or fewer.
- L4 Read it twice, new passage. Pass on read 2: 1 or fewer. Never 0: listeners weigh restarts least, and a quick clean fix beats freezing to avoid one.
- L5 Say it twice: 90 s Impromptu x 2 on the same topic. Pass on take 2: 1 or fewer restarts a minute, the app's restart target. This moves the skill into free talk, where restarts like 'I kinda ner— I'm kinda nervous' happen.

Reading guards: the existing followedScript and 8-skipped-words rules, and at least 120 wpm. Check-backs: an unseen cold read at the level's slip limit (L1 to L4) or a new-topic talk at 1 restart a minute (L5), so cold reads become the transfer test. Checkpoint: 2 minutes on a new topic with 1 or fewer restarts a minute.

Why this works: restarts fade last, dropping only after the 4th repetition in one study (S24; library restart-021), and listeners weigh them least (S33; restart-025).

Long pauses (pauses of 5 s or more, kept honest by the Clean stretch; Impromptu topics). Every level is plan, tell, then tell again. Prep time shrinks, and the retell sometimes gets shorter, 4/3/2 style.
- L1: 30 s to jot 3 points, tell it in 90 s, review, then tell it again in 90 s. Pass on take 2: at most one 5 s+ pause, and a clean stretch of 15 s or more.
- L2: 20 s prep, then 90 s and 75 s. Pass: no 5 s+ pause, clean stretch of 20 s or more.
- L3: 10 s prep, then 90 s and 60 s. Pass: no 5 s+ pause, clean stretch of 30 s or more.
- L4: no prep, then 120 s and 90 s. Pass: no 5 s+ pause, short mid-sentence pauses per minute at or below your baseline, clean stretch of 45 s or more.
- L5: no prep, new topic, 120 s x 2. Pass: no 5 s+ pause, clean stretch of 60 s or more.

Extra guard: fillers plus restarts per minute no more than 1 above baseline, so freezes are not traded for ums. Check-backs: a new topic, no prep, 60 to 90 s, with no 5 s+ pause and the level's clean stretch. Checkpoint: 2 minutes on a new topic with no 5 s+ pause, ideally in the room.

Why this works: retelling the same talk in less time cut pauses and carried over to a new topic (S26; library general-014). Trying hard not to pause just moves the hesitation elsewhere, mostly into repeated words, so plan instead (S47, as corrected below; long-078). Recall your outline from memory (long-060).

Pace (wpm inside the target band: 140 to 160 by default, or the prompt's own band).
- L1 Feel the band: read the ladder passage twice at 150 ± 10 wpm, with Practice's time bar as the pacer. Pass on read 2.
- L2 Shift gears: read it once at 140 ± 10 and once at 160 ± 10, with at most 2 restarts each. Both reads must land.
- L3 Talk in the band: 90 s Impromptu x 2 on the same topic. Pass on take 2: wpm in the band. If your baseline is below the band, take 2 shrinks to 70 s with the same content (a 4/3/2 push); if it is above, take 2 stays 90 s.
- L4 Hold it: 120 s x 2. Pass on take 2: in the band.
- L5 New topic: 120 s x 2 on a new topic. Pass: in the band, with weighted other slips at or below baseline, so you don't rush into ums.

Check-backs: a 60 to 90 s new-topic talk in the band. Crown after L3: Speed ladder at 180 ± 10 wpm with at most 1 restart. Checkpoint: 2 minutes on a new topic in the band.

Why this works: listeners judge fluency mostly by speed and how often you pause (r about .6), and retelling under time pressure raised speed with carry-over (S33, S26; library pause-029, general-014).

### Skill path / levels (the Duolingo-style wrapper) (weak)

No study has tested a level-based skill path for speaking against anything else. The closest analog is a gamified VR speaking app for 13 to 16 year olds: 15 levels, 4 of 5 stars to unlock. It beat a waitlist right after treatment (d = 0.61, p = .015) but not a plain online lesson (p = .056), and there were no group differences at 3 months. At 12 months only the plain lesson plus real-life speaking tasks was still improving (-8.3 points, p = .006), and only 32 of 100 teens answered. Duolingo replaced its skill tree with the path because learners skipped the optional levels, and the path builds in spaced practice. But its speaking report (66% Spanish, 53% French at A2 after Unit 5) has no comparison group. In a Duolingo-funded study of 287 learners, time in the app went with written gains but not oral gains. Reviewing finished levels was the best predictor of oral gains (r = .21, as read by our researcher).

### Unlocks / mastery gating (pass a level to open the next) (moderate)

Mastery learning means reaching a set standard before moving on, with re-practice. It raised exam scores across 108 controlled classroom evaluations. Our researchers read that it helped weaker students more and that self-paced versions often cut college completion rates. Learner control in general (choosing pace, order, practice or review) made about zero difference (g = 0.05, S14 as corrected below); there is no estimate for order alone, so a locked order likely costs little. But passing once during practice is not proof of learning: 'current performance is not a reliable index of learning, which can only be measured by recall after a delay.' That is why cleared levels should be checked again on a later day. None of these studies was about speaking.

### Streaks (mixed)

In a 60,000-student randomized trial, weekly messages highlighting streaks raised platform use, with no discouragement effect. The streak group scored 0.13 to 0.17 SD higher than control on a math test, but only about 1,500 students took that test. The streak group was not significantly better than the plain-reminder groups, and the paper is an unreviewed draft. Across 7 experiments, an intact streak raised later engagement more than a broken one. The drop after a break was bigger when people blamed themselves, and an option to repair the streak softened it. In a habit study of 96 volunteers, missing one day 'did not materially affect' habit formation, which took 18 to 254 days. So streaks help people show up, but a streak that resets after one missed day punishes something that does no harm.

### Gamification: points, XP, ranks, leaderboards (mixed)

Game features beat plain instruction by small to medium amounts:
- knowledge g = 0.49
- motivation g = 0.36
- skills g = 0.25 (9 studies)
- grades in another meta-analysis g = 0.50

The knowledge effect was the stable one in rigorous studies.

The risks:
- Rewards for simply doing or finishing a task lowered later free-choice motivation (d = -0.40 and -0.36). Positive feedback that tells you how you did raised it (d = +0.33).
- Points, levels and a leaderboard raised how much people produced but not its quality (N = 273).
- Practice in a gamified course dipped from about week 4 and partly recovered by weeks 6 to 10.
- Feedback that threatens self-esteem barely worked (d = 0.08, versus 0.47 with little threat), and feedback designed to discourage backfired (d = -0.14).

That last point is a warning about showing a very harsh rank right after each take.

### Specific, challenging goals (strong)

Specific, hard goals beat 'do your best' (d about .42 to .80 across the meta-analyses Locke and Latham summarize). Feedback paired with difficult goals worked better (d = 0.51) than feedback with easy or do-your-best goals (d = 0.30). Feedback effects shrank to nearly nothing on very complex tasks (d = 0.03, versus 0.55 on simple ones). That favors one narrow number per level, like '5 fillers a minute', over a whole-speech score. The evidence comes mostly from adults on work and lab tasks, not speaking.

### Graded difficulty (levels that step up) (moderate)

Graded exposure means climbing a ladder of harder speaking situations. It is the most common treatment in 30 RCTs that reduced fear of public speaking (g = 0.74 after treatment, 1.11 at follow-up). But in one small RCT (40 adults, obsessional thoughts, not speech), varying the difficulty worked as well as a strict ladder. So doing the harder steps matters more than their exact order. The one synthesis of presentation training lists the design of the learning task among its seven principles. Harder practice helps only when the learner can meet it. A person's own successes are by far the strongest source of confidence (61 studies, N = 8,965). Both point to a first level set from the speaker's own starting numbers.

### Repeated practice (say the same talk again) (moderate)

Giving the same talk again is the best-supported fluency drill. The trials are small and use second-language speakers.
- Over 6 repetitions (n = 32), speed rose most in the first 3. Mid-clause pauses fell until the 4th. Self-repairs (restarts) dropped only after the 4th.
- Gains held on later tests only for students who repeated the same talk (n = 24).
- The 4/3/2 activity is the same talk retold in less time each round. It raised speaking speed from 2.67 to 3.13 syllables per second (d = 1.54) and cut mid-clause pauses from .21 to .10 (d = 0.96), and the gains carried over to a new topic days later. Repairs did not change in any group (n = 36).
- One immediate repeat raised speed but did not reduce filled pauses (n = 71).

Practice matters, but it explains less than once claimed: 4% of performance differences in education. Library entries: restart-021, pause-021, general-014, pause-022, filler-079.

### Immediate automated feedback (moderate)

Feedback helps learning on average (d = 0.48, 435 studies), and what it says matters most:
- Feedback that explains what went wrong and how to fix it had d = 0.99. Bare reward or punishment had 0.24.
- Over a third of feedback interventions made performance worse, more so when they turned attention to the self.
- Feedback on change since the last try did better (0.55 versus 0.28), and computer-given feedback held up (0.41).

For speaking specifically:
- The largest RCT of an automated presentation coach (180 students) found a small but significant gain rated by experts. The gain was uneven across skills.
- Its authors later wrote that filled pauses 'may require more practice and possibly different types of feedback.' Gains came mainly from lower performers. Only four effectiveness studies of such coaches exist.
- A personalized report after the take helped. Live audience reactions made practice feel more real but did not improve results.
- In small studies, a filler report after each session cut fillers by 30%. Adding a live cue cut them by 50%, or kept them lower after a break, but some speakers found it startling or distracting.

### Self-review of recordings and awareness training (moderate)

Reviewing recordings of yourself improved professionals' communication skills (ES 0.40, 33 studies). The effect was much bigger with a structured checklist of target skills (0.55 versus 0.21). It built wanted behaviours better than it removed unwanted ones (0.41 versus 0.28). In 12 public-speaking course studies, video feedback improved speeches without a significant rise in anxiety. Students who had to rate their recorded talk on a rubric did better on the next talk, though their self-scores did not match the instructor's.

Awareness training means learning to notice each um, often swapping in a short silent pause. It cut fillers for nearly every participant across 7 small single-case studies (2016 to 2022). Describing your own fillers precisely was enough for 4 of 9 speakers; others needed to spot them on video or live.

The limits:
- Booster sessions were needed.
- The replacement pause faded for most speakers.
- Carry-over to a real class showed for 1 of 4.
- In a 2-person study, fewer fillers did not raise expert ratings.
- Self-assessment alone gave smaller gains than teacher feedback (144 students).

Library entries: filler-087, filler-088, filler-089, filler-091, filler-052, filler-085, filler-086, filler-093, pause-075.

### Modelling exemplars (see or hear a good example first) (weak)

The presentation-training synthesis lists modelling, meaning watching a good example, as one of its seven principles. The direct test found that watching models before practising beat practice alone on only a limited number of criteria (Belgian undergraduates, sample size not reported). The cheapest form that fits this app is a precise description with an example and a non-example of each slip. Awareness training starts that way, and it was enough on its own for 4 of 9 speakers. Library filler-053 is a self-paced audio-example version, but it is a poster, not peer-reviewed.

### Spacing (practice spread over days, coming back later) (moderate)

Spacing beats cramming for remembering words (839 assessments), and the best gap grows with how long you need to remember. For skills, the benefit is large for simple motor tasks (d = 0.97) but near zero for complex ones (d = 0.07 to 0.11), and weaker studies inflated it (1.22 versus 0.40). In speech, a one-week gap between repeats gave the biggest drop in filled pauses (n = 71; the groups differed at the start). On Duolingo, reviewing finished levels predicted oral gains better than time in the app did. Mixing different skills in one session gave almost no transfer benefit for under-18s (SMD 0.12). So keep one skill per level and let later check-backs do the mixing. Library: filler-079, long-060.

### Time pressure and sudden death (mixed)

Time pressure helps when it comes with repetition: retelling the same talk in less time sped speakers up and cut pauses, with carry-over to new topics. Zero-tolerance pressure is the risky part:
- When speakers were punished for pausing, they paused less but said more fillers and repeated more words. The authors conclude that the task's planning load sets the total amount of hesitation.
- Making speakers more self-conscious or more afraid of being judged raised their ums sharply.
- Teens told to monitor how they came across felt more anxious, and partners and observers rated them worse (57 adolescents, conversation task).
- High perfectionism predicted poorer outcomes in a digital program for teens with public speaking anxiety.

And zero is not needed: listeners did not mark speakers down at 5 fillers a minute, but 12 um/uh a minute hurt. No study tested a sudden-death mode directly. Library: long-078, filler-094, filler-095, filler-061.

### Anxiety (mixed)

What helps fear of public speaking:
- Graded exposure, practising harder speaking situations step by step, works well: 30 RCTs, g = 0.74 after treatment and 1.11 at follow-up. Technology-delivered versions worked as well as face-to-face, but only 1 trial studied adolescents.
- A fully self-guided internet program worked as well as the same program led by a therapist, and the gains held for a year (n = 127).
- Skills drills alone were the weakest anxiety treatment (r = .14, versus .37 for desensitization and .33 for changing anxious thoughts). Effects added up when methods were combined (181 experiments, a 1987 dissertation).

What that means for the Path:
- The gamified teen VR path was not better than a plain lesson. Real-life speaking tasks were what kept improving at 12 months.
- Stress makes people pause more, especially strong stress responders, so the long-pause count works as a nerves gauge (library long-059).
- A delivery-drill path alone will do little for anxiety. Adding steps in front of a real listener, and a cue to focus on the listener rather than yourself, fits the evidence better.

## Caveats

**How to read the numbers:** d or g is the gap between groups in standard deviations: about 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. r is a correlation from 0 to 1. ES and SMD are other names for an effect size on the same scale as d.

**This design is not proven.** No study tests this exact design. The "Loop Path" is built from ingredients that have evidence. The app should say "built on tested practice methods", never "scientifically proven".

**Who was studied:**
- Mostly adults, university students and second-language learners.
- Many speech studies are single-case (2 to 9 people) or small RCTs (24 to 57 people), followed for days to weeks.
- Only 1 of 30 public-speaking-anxiety RCTs studied adolescents.
- No study looked at high-school debaters, impromptu rounds or debate judges.

**What was measured:** listener or expert ratings, second-language fluency ratios, or self-reported anxiety. None used the app's exact counts. In one 2-person study, fewer fillers did not make speakers look better to experts. The path trains delivery fluency only; argument and content are not scored.

**The design numbers are choices, not tested values.** These were not tested:
- 4 days a week
- check-backs at +2 and +7 days
- the 'halfway' level 1
- the guards (+1 slip a minute, 80% talk time, 120 wpm)
- the 8/6/5/3 filler ladder
- the prep times and take lengths
- five levels instead of Duolingo's three

The studies informed them. The 14-day benchmark should be used to tune them.

**Not confirmed on the primary page by me:**
- Kulik 1990's pooled effect size, which is not in the abstract. The 'stronger for weaker students' and 'lower completion' details come from the researchers' reading.
- Locke and Latham's d ranges, seen in a search excerpt of the paper.
- The Kluger and DeNisi moderator values, read from Hattie and Timperley 2007, Table 3.
- Sudina and Plonsky's r values. The abstract confirms time in the app went with written but not oral gains.
- Chollet's and WSCoach's arm-level numbers and sample sizes. The abstracts confirm the direction.
- Ochoa and Dominguez's effect size (paywalled).
- Allen 1987's r values, from an OCR-scanned table.
- Karich 2014's g = 0.05, seen in a search excerpt of the abstract.
- Czyż 2024's figures; the paper has internal inconsistencies.

**Source quality:**
- Aulagnon 2026 is an unreviewed working paper whose learning result rests on about 1,500 test takers.
- The Duolingo posts are vendor reports with no comparison group.
- Sudina and Plonsky was Duolingo-funded and observational.
- Bjork and Bjork is a narrative chapter.
- Allen 1987 is an unpublished dissertation.
- Kahlon 2023 was underpowered, and Hauge 2026 kept only 32 of 100 participants.
- The awareness-training studies are single-case designs.
- The Perrin poster (library filler-053) is not peer-reviewed.

**Owner decisions:**
- **Replay audio.** 'Spot it' and 'Play 3 s' need take 1's audio held in memory during the loop and then dropped. The app currently says audio is never stored. If no replay is wanted, the review falls back to the transcript, which is a weaker version of the tested video-spotting step.
- **Rank.** Moving the harsh rank off the Path header and out of the loop goes against the plan's 'tier badge at the top'. It is a suggestion based on the self-esteem-threat evidence (S16, S50), and the owner decides.
- **Live tapping and live cues** are optional because the small studies found them helpful but distracting, and in one study of teens, turning attention inward together with safety behaviours raised anxiety (S49; the study cannot separate the two).

**Other limits:**
- Debate speed norms were not studied; 140 to 160 wpm is the app's default band.
- The app is a practice tool, not an anxiety treatment. The exposure evidence comes from treatment programs.
